Rocky Mountain Fire Tower

May 20, 2026 — 18:07

A few years ago, I came across a copy of “Parks and Recreation Structures” by Albert Good while working at my first architecture job. I was immediately hooked by the heavy, tree trunk columns and the fantasy of rugged, Western living. I used to look through all the different sections of the book: Housing, Communal Structures, Administration Buildings, etc. and dream about how we could build something similar today.

Fast forward a few years during the Covid-19 pandemic, and I was looking for things to do outside that didn’t put me in any immediate danger of getting Covid. I hatched the idea to visit some of the parks building in Texas near Austin and San Antonio. I and a friend visited Palmetto State Park, Bastrop State Park, Lake Corpus Cristi State, and a few others. It was interesting to see the different interpretation of rugged, outdoors themed buildings suited for Texas and not a dry, alpine forest. The stone was local red sandstone, and the wood was either cypress or palmetto branches draped across roof structures. I gained a valuable lesson in scale from visiting these buildings. I saw they were quite well balanced and scaled to themselves. Once I got closer, I saw that a lot of architectural elements seemed oversized and comical. It wasn’t a bad design decision and didn’t negatively affect the experience of the spaces, rather, it demonstrated how to balance the rustic material with scale.

I moved to Colorado in 2023. Most of my first year living in Colorado was spent skiing and rock climbing every day or weekend. I eventually reignited my interest in the rustic Parks buildings in 2025. I recalled seeing a fire tower in Colorado in my 1923 Edition of Good’s book. A quick google search said that Shadow Mountain Fire Tower was on the shores of Grand Lake in Rocky Mountain National Park, only an hour and a half away. I decided to go to Shadow Mountain in mid-spring once the snow and ice had melted and the park was open fully.

The Shadow Mountain Fire Tower was constructed in 1933. The site had hosted a fire watch site since 1930, after which it was decided a permanent structure should be on the site. The tower was built as a group of fire detection towers in RMNP, which were some of the earliest fire protection methods employed by the NPS. Today, Shadow Mountain Fire Tower is the only remaining tower standing.

The building is around 17 feet square and is three stories tall. The first two stories of the tower are uncoursed stone which house a visitor center and museum on the ground floor, and a quarter for the employee manning the fire tower on the second floor. The third floor was the observation deck area, which was surrounded by windows, making it suitable for fire watching. All the floors above the ground floor are accessed by a log and plank walkway and stair, with the structure forming a balcony wrapping the entire third floor observation deck. All the doors are wood board and batten, and all the handrails and balusters are logs of various sizes. Construction was exceedingly difficult as most of the building supplies, save for logs, wooden shingles, wood framing, and stone, had to be carted on mules up to the 9,923-foot summit.

In his excerpt on Shadow Mountain in “Parks and Recreation Structures”, Good said that Shadow Mountain was “illustrative of the happy results where a masonry structure can appear to grow out of a natural rock outcrop.” From my trip there, I would have to agree. I found Shadow Mountain to be one of the most compelling Parks Structures I had visited. I could see all the material in its raw tree form around the tower and noted how the rocks around the tower matched the hue and luster of the ones used in the towers rock base.

It was interesting to compare Shadow Mountain to various modernist projects that seem to share its lone placement among nature. Very often I see a lot of lauded modernist architectural projects and think that they gain quite a bit by being in such a beautiful location. A lot of the more unsavory parts of the designs are smoothed over by the surrounding beauty and irregularity inherent in nature. They don’t really belong there, but it seems ok because of the beauty around it. Shadow Mountain on the other hand, while not also belonging, does synchronize with its surroundings a lot better. Instead of using nature to smooth over its slightly discordant aesthetic it uses nature as a muse to blend in and become one with the landscape.

My series of drawings were generated from my visit there in Spring of 2026. I did the hour-long hike solo as dogs weren’t allowed in RMNP (The Tower is accessed through a section of National Forest where dogs are allowed but the Tower itself is in RMNP). I spent about 2 hours at the summit taking measurements, some of which requiring me to lean quite far over some barriers. A minor snowstorm coaxed me back down to my car after about 2 hours. Any holes in measurements I was able to piece together from photos from back in the day. I later did my drawing set based on those measurements and did a watercolor rendering as well.
